Karen Lee at home with the paintings

In conversation

Karen Lee

Q — What made you want to live with this work?

I saw it at the opening and couldn't stop looking at it. It wasn't one thing — it was the colour, but also the writing in the paint. Something about having words inside a painting felt like a secret I was being let in on.

Q — Has the painting changed for you over time?

Every morning it's slightly different depending on the light. Some days it feels almost quiet. Other days it's extremely loud — and I mean that in the best possible way. I don't think I've finished looking at it yet.

Q — What would you say to someone considering a print?

That the energy comes through. I know that sounds like something a person just says. But something of the original feeling stays in. The scale, the looseness — it doesn't shrink the way you'd expect. I'd say trust it.

The artist

Mousumi Saha

Mousumi Saha

Mo Saha (b.1980) is a painter born in Middlesbrough, made in London and has recently relocated her studio to Portugal. Her journey from portraiture to contemporary landscape and flower painting reflects a quest for insight, beauty and joy.

Inspired by recent encounters with flora and the natural world and personal life events, Saha delights in a quest to create brief moments of sanctuary and lightness to counter the heaviness that will on occasion pervade our lives. In contrast to Saha's formal training in drawing portraits from life, she revels in the unpredictability, imperfection and spontaneity of spray paint, drips and splashes, all of which are whole-heartedly embraced. The classical limited palette and canvas grounds have also been replaced by baby blues, cotton candy and neon pinks, reminiscent of carefree childhood states, before the notion of 'taste' has kicked in. Saha beckons viewers into a realm brimming with innocence, and boundless joy.

Rose symbolism has existed as a metaphor for cosmic consciousness since the birth of world religions from Sufism and Hinduism to Christianity. The suspension of the flowers in a dream-like sky create a certain dimension of divinity which the painter felt was encapsulated in the poem 'The Sick-Bed' by Rabindranath Tagore. The painter's recent experiences of health issues herself and in her family have further defined that her biggest source of delight over despair has been witnessing simple moments of life force in action, primarily in the close observation of flowers in bloom. The artist also unashamedly embraces the decorative, romantic and feminine associations the rose evokes — a motif ever present in chintzy wallpaper and Rococo-inspired show plates.

"I can only gaze and see / in the sky / the spreading layers / of a vast, radiant, petal'd rose"— Rabindranath Tagore, 'The Sick Bed'

At the heart of the exhibition lies a profound invitation: to rediscover the simple pleasures of existence and embrace the transformative power of art. As Saha explains, "I refer to these paintings as 'joyspaces' — a state I generate for myself through my studio practice. It is my wish that people who see this work find and create their own joyspace, fostering moments of genuine joy and insight. I have come to know that to reach those states often requires an element of risk or jeopardy. It can be scary yet exhilarating."
